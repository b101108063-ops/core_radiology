```
Upper extremity angiography
Normal anatomy of the upper extremity vasculature
Arterial anatomy of the upper extremity

- The aortic arch gives rise to three great vessels: the innominate (brachiocephalic) artery, the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian artery.
- The right subclavian artery arises from the brachiocephalic artery. The left subclavian artery arises directly from the aortic arch.
- The subclavian artery becomes the axillary artery at the lateral border of the first rib.
- The axillary artery becomes the brachial artery at the inferior border of the teres major muscle.
- The brachial artery bifurcates into the radial and ulnar arteries at the proximal forearm.
- The radial and ulnar arteries form the deep and superficial palmar arches, respectively.
- The dominant arterial supply to the hand is the superficial palmar arch (from the ulnar artery).

Subclavian steal syndrome

- Subclavian steal syndrome occurs when there is stenosis or occlusion of the subclavian artery proximal to the origin of the vertebral artery, causing retrograde flow in the vertebral artery to supply the arm.
- The most common cause is atherosclerosis, typically at the left subclavian artery origin.
- Patients may be asymptomatic or present with vertebrobasilar insufficiency symptoms (such as dizziness or syncope) especially with arm exercise.
- Imaging shows reversal of flow in the vertebral artery on duplex ultrasound.
- Treatment is endovascular stenting or surgical bypass (carotid-subclavian or subclavian-subclavian bypass).
```
