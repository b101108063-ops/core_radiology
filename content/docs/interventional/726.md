Vascular trauma
Penetrating trauma

- Penetrating trauma to the extremities can injure arteries, veins, or both. Penetrating injuries are more commonly isolated arterial injuries compared to blunt trauma.
- Hard signs of arterial injury requiring immediate intervention include expanding hematoma, pulsatile bleeding, audible bruit, palpable thrill, and acute ischemia.
- Soft signs of arterial injury include nonexpanding hematoma, stable hematoma, history of moderate bleeding, and decreased pulses.
- Duplex ultrasound, CTA, or conventional angiography can be used to evaluate for vascular injury.
- Penetrating injuries with hard signs go directly to surgery without imaging.
- Treatment of arterial injury includes surgical repair (lateral arteriorrhaphy, resection with end-to-end anastomosis, or interposition graft) or endovascular repair (covered stent placement).

Blunt trauma

- Blunt trauma can cause arterial dissection, pseudoaneurysm, or transection.
- Popliteal artery injury is the most common arterial injury in knee dislocation.
- Iatrogenic arterial injury can occur during orthopedic, vascular, or other surgical procedures.
- Blunt injuries are more commonly associated with arteriovenous fistula (AVF) formation compared to penetrating trauma.

Hemodialysis access
Hemodialysis access overview

- Hemodialysis requires a vascular access site with high blood flow (at least 400 mL/min for effective dialysis).
- The three main types of hemodialysis access are: Autogenous arteriovenous fistula (AVF); Arteriovenous graft (AVG); Tunneled dialysis catheter.
- An autogenous AVF is the preferred access type as it has the lowest infection and thrombosis rates and longest patency.
- The most common AVF is a radiocephalic fistula at the wrist (Brescia-Cimino fistula).
- Other common AVFs include brachiocephalic fistula (at the elbow) and brachial artery to axillary vein fistula.
- An AVG uses a synthetic graft (e.g., PTFE) to create the arteriovenous connection. AVGs can be straight or looped.
- AVGs are used when native veins are inadequate for an AVF.
- Tunneled dialysis catheters are placed in the internal jugular or subclavian vein and used when AVF/AVG is not available or while awaiting maturation of a newly created fistula.
