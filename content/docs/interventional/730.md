Miscellaneous interventional topics
Central venous access

- Central venous catheters (CVCs) are placed for chemotherapy, total parenteral nutrition, hemodialysis, and fluid/medication administration.
- Common insertion sites include internal jugular vein, subclavian vein, and femoral vein.
- Ultrasound guidance is recommended for all central venous placements.
- Tunneled catheters (e.g., Hickman) have a cuff that promotes tissue ingrowth and reduce infection risk.
- Implanted ports are fully implanted devices with a subcutaneous reservoir.
- Complications of CVC placement include pneumothorax, arterial puncture, infection, thrombosis, and catheter malposition.

Chemotherapy embolization

- Chemotherapy embolization ( chemoembolization ) delivers chemotherapy directly to a tumor via its arterial supply, combined with particle embolization to trap the drug and ischemic tumor.
- Most commonly performed for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver metastases.
- Drug-eluting beads (DEB) are particles that slowly release chemotherapy (e.g., doxorubicin) over time.
-DEB-TACE has been shown to be as effective as conventional TACE with fewer systemic side effects.
- Side effects include post-embolization syndrome (pain, fever, nausea) and liver dysfunction.

Radioembolization (Y-90)

- Radioembolization involves injection of Yttrium-90 microspheres directly into the hepatic arterial supply of a liver tumor.
- Y-90 is a beta-emitting isotope with a half-life of 64 hours.
- Radioembolization is used for HCC and liver metastases, particularly from colorectal cancer and neuroendocrine tumors.
- Pre-procedure workup includes hepatic angiography to map the hepatic vasculature and identify extrahepatic vessels that need coil embolization to prevent nontarget deposition.
- MAA (macroaggregated albumin) scan is performed to calculate the lung shunt fraction and estimate radiation dose to the lungs and tumor.
- Complications include radiation hepatitis, GI ulceration, radiation pneumonitis (if lung shunt is high), and cholecystitis.

Ablation therapies
Radiofrequency ablation (RFA)

- RFA uses heat generated by alternating electrical current to destroy tissue.
- Most commonly used for liver tumors (HCC and metastases), renal cell carcinoma, and lung tumors.
- Can be performed percutaneously under CT or ultrasound guidance, or during surgery.
- Complications include pain, fever, hemorrhage, and damage to adjacent structures.

Microwave ablation (MWA)

- MWA uses microwave energy to create thermal destruction of tissue.
- Has several advantages over RFA including higher temperatures, larger ablation zones, and faster ablation times.
- Used for similar indications as RFA.

Cryoablation

- Cryoablation uses extreme cold to destroy tissue.
- A cryoprobe is placed into the tumor and freezing is performed in cycles (freeze-thaw-freeze).
- Allows visualization of the ice ball on CT, providing precise control of the ablation zone.
- Used for renal cell carcinoma, prostate cancer, and bone lesions.
- Complications include cryoshock (severe inflammatory response) and damage to adjacent structures.
